\section{The Core Question}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{What We Are Studying}

As AI systems become more capable and autonomous, a critical safety question emerges. Does an AI
agent \emph{act consistently with what it internally knows}? A model might internally represent the correct state of the world while producing actions that are inconsistent with that representation.
This discrepancy is what we call the \textbf{Belief-Action Gap}.

The question is important for two reasons. First, from an interpretability perspective if internal representations are inconsistent with behaviour, then behavioural evaluation alone is insufficient for understanding the system. Second, from a safety perspective deceptive alignment requires precisely this, a model that internally knows the right thing to do but acts differently, potentially to avoid modification or appear less capable.

\subsection{Formal Setting}

We study an LLM agent (GPT-OSS-20B) navigating a key-door gridworld. The environment is fully
observable to the experimenter but the agent receives only text renderings of the grid.

\textbf{State} The complete world description is a four-tuple:
\[
s = (x, y, \texttt{has\_key}, \texttt{door\_open}) \in \mathcal{S}
\]
where $(x,y)$ is position, and the binary variables encode task phase.

\textbf{History} The agent's interaction history at step $t$ is $h_t = (o_{1:t}, a_{1:t-1})$,
where $o_t$ is the text observation and $a_t$ is the action taken.

\textbf{Actions} $\mathcal{A} = \{\texttt{LEFT}, \texttt{RIGHT}, \texttt{UP}, \texttt{DOWN}\}$.

\textbf{Transition} The deterministic environment transition is $s_{t+1} = f(s_t, a_t)$.

% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{The Three-Component Framework}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The Belief-Action Gap requires three theoretical objects, none of which are directly observable
inside the model. We operationalise each from the model's activations and behaviour.

\[
\underbrace{\hat{q}(s|h_t)}_{\text{(1) Belief}} \quad + \quad
\underbrace{C_\theta(s)}_{\text{(2) Cost function}} \quad + \quad
\underbrace{\hat{p}(s'|s,a)}_{\text{(3) Transition model}}
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
\underbrace{\Delta_t}_{\text{Gap}}
\]

\subsection{Component 1: Belief Representation}

The agent's internal belief is modelled as a distribution over states given history:
\[
\hat{q}(s_t | h_t) \approx \hat{q}(x_t, y_t | h_t) \cdot \hat{q}(\texttt{has\_key}_t | h_t)
\cdot \hat{q}(\texttt{door\_open}_t | h_t)
\]
This factorisation assumes the three state components are approximately independent in the
model's internal representation.

Each factor is estimated by a \textbf{linear probe},  a logistic regression classifier trained on the 8640-dimensional activation vector extracted from different layers at the relevant token
(the last input token before generation begins). Using pre-reasoning activations is deliberate:
they capture what the model has \emph{encoded} about the world before committing to an output.

In addition to these white-box activation probes, we use \textbf{black-box behavioural probes}
as a complementary validation layer. These probes query the model directly about the current
state or the immediate consequence of a proposed action from a single rendered grid state,
without access to activations. Their role is narrower than the white-box probes: they test
whether local state information is explicitly recoverable from behaviour itself, and whether
action errors are accompanied by explicit local belief errors.
They do not estimate the full latent distribution $\hat{q}(s_t \mid h_t)$; rather, they elicit
selected local marginals or relations from a single rendered state.

\subsection{Component 2: Cost Function via IRL}

We assume the agent selects actions according to a Boltzmann policy:
\[
P_\theta(a | s) = \frac{\exp(-\beta \, C_\theta(f(s, a)))}
                       {\sum_{a' \in \mathcal{A}} \exp(-\beta \, C_\theta(f(s, a')))}
\]
where $\beta > 0$ controls rationality and $C_\theta(s)$ is an unknown cost function.
We consider two parameterisations:
\begin{align}
C_\theta(s) &= \theta^\top \phi(s) \quad \text{(linear)} \label{eq:linear}\\
C_\theta(s) &= g_\theta(\phi(s)) \quad \text{(MLP)} \label{eq:mlp}
\end{align}
We recover $\theta^*$ by maximising the log-likelihood of observed actions:
\[
\theta^* = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_\theta \sum_{t=1}^T \log P_\theta(a_t | s_t^*)
\]

\subsection{Component 3: Internal Transition Model}

The true environment transition is deterministic: $s_{t+1} = f(s_t, a_t)$.
However, the agent may internally operate with a different effective model:
\[
\hat{p}(s' | s, a) \neq \mathbf{1}\{s' = f(s, a)\}
\]
We estimate $\hat{p}$ by prompting the model with hypothetical actions and observing its
predicted next state from $K$ samples:
\[
\hat{p}(s' | s, a) = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \mathbf{1}\{\hat{s}'^{(k)} = s'\}
\]
In practice, our action-conditioned behavioural probes should be read as local diagnostics of
immediate consequences, not as a full reconstruction of $\hat{p}(s' \mid s,a)$ over the whole
state space.

\subsection{The Belief-Action Gap}

Combining all three components, the expected cost of action $a$ under the agent's own internal
representation is:
\[
\boxed{J_t(a) = \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} \hat{q}(s | h_t)
               \sum_{s' \in \mathcal{S}} \hat{p}(s' | s, a) \, C_\theta(s')}
\]
The \textbf{Belief-Action Gap} at step $t$ is then:
\[
\boxed{\Delta_t = J_t(a_t) - \min_{a \in \mathcal{A}} J_t(a)}
\]

$\Delta_t = 0$ means the agent's chosen action is optimal under its own beliefs and values.
$\Delta_t > 0$ means the agent took an action that, by its own lights, was suboptimal, a
direct measure of internal inconsistency.
The behavioural wall-gap metrics reported later are narrower than $\Delta_t$: they are
single-step, action-relevant lower-bound diagnostics of local inconsistency, not substitutes
for the full white-box quantity above.

% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Feature Representations: $\phi(s)$}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

A central design choice is the feature vector $\phi(s)$ used to represent states for the cost
function. We have explored two approaches.

\subsection{Activation Features}

The model's own hidden activations serve as the feature vector. We build a table as for each state $s$, we average activations across all trajectory visits:
\[
\phi(s) = \frac{1}{N_s} \sum_{t:\, s_t = s} h_t
\]
where $N_s$ is the number of times state $s$ was visited. This cancels trajectory-specific
context effects and gives a canonical representation of the state.

\textbf{Dimensionality:} $\phi(s) \in \mathbb{R}^{8640}$ (three adjacent token positions, each
2880-dimensional at layer 15).

\textbf{Key property:} The phi-table enables \emph{exact counterfactual evaluation}. For any
action $a$, the feature vector $\phi(f(s,a))$ can be looked up directly without proxies.

\subsection{Hand-Crafted GT Features}

To test whether the cost function encodes task-relevant structure rather than grid-specific
patterns, we define a 5-dimensional grid-invariant feature vector:
\[
\phi_{\text{GT}}(s) = \begin{pmatrix}
d_{\text{BFS}}(\text{agent}, \text{goal}) \\
d_{\text{BFS}}(\text{agent}, \text{key}) \cdot \mathbf{1}[\neg\texttt{has\_key}] \\
d_{\text{BFS}}(\text{agent}, \text{door}) \cdot \mathbf{1}[\neg\texttt{door\_open}] \\
\mathbf{1}[\texttt{has\_key}] \\
\mathbf{1}[\texttt{door\_open}]
\end{pmatrix}
\]
where $d_{\text{BFS}}$ denotes BFS shortest path distance through the grid. These features
are \emph{grid-invariant}: they encode task progress semantics that transfer across different
grid layouts. The intuition is that ``closer to goal = good, having key = good'' regardless of
the specific wall configuration.

% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{IRL Methods: One-Step vs. MaxEnt}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{One-Step IRL}

The one-step approach treats each $(s_t, a_t)$ transition independently. The gradient of the
log-likelihood at each transition is:
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} = \phi(f(s_t, a_t)) - \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \pi_\theta(a|s_t) \cdot \phi(f(s_t, a))
\]
This is the MaxEnt IRL gradient in the special case where state visitation frequencies
are not computed globally, instead each transition is treated as if it were the only one.

\textbf{Advantages:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Works when each trajectory uses a different grid (no global value iteration needed)
\item Can use full 8640-dimensional activation features directly
\item Fast: no backward pass required
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Limitations:}
\begin{itemize}
\item The learned $C_\theta$ is not guaranteed to be Bellman-consistent (it is an arbitrary function, not a proper value function)
\item Loses long-horizon credit assignment
\item Is an action predictor rather than a reward function recovery method
\end{itemize}

\subsection{MaxEnt IRL}

The proper MaxEnt IRL algorithm recovers a reward function
$R_\theta(s) = \theta^\top \phi(s)$ by solving:
\[
\theta^* = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_\theta \sum_{\tilde\zeta} \log P(\tilde\zeta|\theta,T)
\]
subject to the feature count constraint $\mathbb{E}_\theta[\phi] = \tilde{f}$.

The algorithm proceeds as follows:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{MaxEnt IRL with Value Iteration}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Initialise $\theta \leftarrow \mathbf{0}$
\Repeat
\State Compute reward: $R(s) = \theta^\top \phi(s)$ for all $s$
\State \textbf{Backward pass} (value iteration, $N$ iterations):
\State \quad $Z_{s_{\text{terminal}}} \leftarrow 1$
\State \quad $Z_{a,s} \leftarrow e^{R(s)} \cdot Z_{s'}$ where $s' = T(s,a)$
\State \quad $Z_s \leftarrow \sum_a Z_{a,s}$
\State \quad $Q(s,a) \leftarrow R(s) + V(T(s,a))$ where $V(s) = \log\sum_a \exp Q(s,a)$
\State \quad $\pi(a|s) \leftarrow Z_{a,s} / Z_s$
\State \textbf{Forward pass} (state visitation frequencies):
\State \quad $D_{s_0} \leftarrow P(s = s_{\text{initial}})$
\State \quad $D_{s'} \leftarrow \sum_s \sum_a D_s \cdot \pi(a|s) \cdot \mathbf{1}[T(s,a)=s']$
\State Gradient: $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} = \tilde{f} - \sum_s D_s \phi(s)$
\State $\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}$
\Until{convergence}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The resulting $V(s) = \log\sum_a \exp Q(s,a)$ satisfies the soft Bellman equation:
\[
V(s) = R(s) + \log\sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \exp(V(T(s,a)))
\]
with boundary condition $V(s_{\text{terminal}}) = 0$. This is a valid value function, unlike
the arbitrary function learned by one-step IRL.

\textbf{Practical consideration:} Because our trajectories come from a fixed grid, value iteration is feasible. For multiple grids, each grid needs its own backward/forward
pass, but with PCA-compressed features (20-dim) this remains tractable.

\subsection{Why the Distinction Matters}

\begin{insight}
One-step IRL answers: ``Can we predict the next action from the cost of successor states?''\\
MaxEnt IRL answers: ``Is there a reward function $R(s)$ such that the optimal policy under $R$
explains the agent's behaviour?''

\end{insight}

\section{The A* Optimal Policy Baseline}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

To assess how rational the agent is, we compute the optimal cost function analytically:
\[
C_{A^*}(s) = d_{A^*}(s, \text{task completion})
\]
where $d_{A^*}$ is the minimum number of steps to complete the task from state $s$:
\[
C_{A^*}(s) = \begin{cases}
d_{\text{BFS}}(\text{agent}, \text{goal}) & \text{if door\_open} \\
d_{\text{BFS}}(\text{agent}, \text{door}) + d_{\text{BFS}}(\text{door}, \text{goal}) & \text{if has\_key, door locked} \\
d_{\text{BFS}}(\text{agent}, \text{key}) + d_{\text{BFS}}(\text{key}, \text{door}) + d_{\text{BFS}}(\text{door}, \text{goal}) & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]
The A* policy then predicts whichever action minimises the cost of the next state. The
A* policy accuracy (how often A*'s recommendation matches the agent's actual action) provides
an \emph{objective upper bound} on rational behaviour.

\paragraph{Setup and metrics.}
Each behavioural probe is a \emph{single-step, history-free} query. The prompt contains only
the current rendered grid and, when relevant, the agent's carrying-key status; no previous
observations, answers, or trajectory history are included. We used three probe families:
directional wall probes, asking whether the neighbouring cell in a cardinal direction is
blocked; object-direction probes, asking whether the goal, key, or door is immediately left,
right, up, or down of the agent; and action-conditioned probes, asking about the immediate
consequence of a proposed move. For every probe we report accuracy against environment ground
truth and valid parse rate. For wall probes we additionally report two narrower
action-facing diagnostics: a \emph{local contradiction rate}, which asks how often the model
moves into a direction it explicitly reports as blocked, and an \emph{A$^*$-conditioned gap
rate}, which asks how often the observed action is still non-optimal under the DoorKey-aware
A$^*$ baseline despite a correct wall judgment. The denominators in these rates are eligible
\emph{single-step states}, not trajectories.
For goal, key, and door probes we treat the results as belief-accuracy measures only, since
these probes do not by themselves define a unique one-step action contradiction.

\paragraph{Results.}
The smoke-test stage showed that, once prompt ambiguity and answer-extraction issues were
removed, the interface itself was stable: parse rates were essentially perfect and the
remaining errors were local rather than global. On the balanced trajectory-derived
failure-mode subset, directional wall probes remained highly accurate (mean greedy accuracy
$0.922$, Monte Carlo accuracy $0.969$). Object-direction probes were also strong overall
(mean greedy accuracy $0.880$, Monte Carlo accuracy $0.979$), but less uniform across probe
types: goal-direction probes were perfect on this subset, door-direction probes were
near-perfect, and key-direction probes were clearly harder, with greedy accuracies between
$0.50$ and $0.81$ depending on direction. On the expanded wall-focused slice of 33 tagged
failure states, directional wall accuracy remained high (greedy accuracy between $0.91$ and
$0.97$ by direction), yet the action-facing contradiction metrics were non-zero.

\begin{result}
The clearest behavioural belief-action gap appears on wall-hit states. On the expanded
wall-focused slice, the model correctly reported that the chosen direction was blocked and
nevertheless took that move in $3/3$ eligible wall-hit cases. More broadly, the
A$^*$-conditioned wall-gap rate remained substantial for \texttt{RIGHT} ($4/6$ eligible
states) and \texttt{UP} ($5/10$ eligible states), showing that many suboptimal actions are
not explained by a failure to state the relevant local wall fact.
\end{result}

\paragraph{Interpretation and role in the paper.}
These behavioural probes do \emph{not} establish the full belief-action gap on their own.
They do not recover the full latent distribution $\hat{q}(s\mid h_t)$, and for non-wall
facts such as key or goal direction they do not by themselves determine what action the model
ought to take. Their value is narrower and more conservative: before appealing to latent
activations, they already identify states where the model explicitly states an action-relevant
fact correctly and still acts against it. Methodologically, this validates that the textual
environment serialisation supports reliable elicitation of local beliefs. Substantively, it
strengthens the motivation for the white-box analysis that follows: if even explicit
state-conditional judgements can diverge from behaviour, then activation-level probes and IRL
are needed to determine whether the deeper internal objects $\hat{q}$, $\hat{p}$, and
$C_\theta$ are also misaligned.

\section{Extension to the Cheat Card Game}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Why Cheat?}

The gridworld provides a controlled setting but the agent has no incentive to be deceptive, it simply navigates toward a goal. The card game \textit{Cheat} (also known as \textit{BS} or
\textit{I Doubt It}) provides a natural extension that preserves all structural properties of our
framework while introducing genuine deception as a core mechanic rather than an imposed condition.

The game satisfies four key desiderata simultaneously:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Natural deception:} Deception is not an option the agent may choose, it is the
central strategic mechanism. The agent must decide when to lie and when to tell the truth as a
function of its hand, its model of opponents, and the current game state. There is no analogue
of this in the gridworld.

\item \textbf{Fixed MDP:} Unlike the multi-grid setting where each trajectory uses a different
wall layout making global value iteration intractable, Cheat has one fixed ruleset. The state
space is well-defined and stable across all trajectories, making proper MaxEnt IRL with value
iteration feasible.

\item \textbf{Already POMDP:} Partial observability is built into the game structure, players
cannot see opponents' hands. This is not artificially imposed but is a natural feature of the
environment, making it a clean testbed for belief-action gap measurement under genuine uncertainty.

\item \textbf{Causal effect of actions:} Each action directly modifies the pile (cards played)
and the risk/reward structure (pile size that the loser of a challenge must take). This is more
causally direct than games with random elements like poker flops, where the action's effect is
mediated by chance.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Rules of Cheat}

Players are dealt a standard 52-card deck. On each turn, a player places one or more cards
face-down on a central pile and \emph{claims} they are a specific rank. Claims must follow a fixed rank sequence (e.g., Ace, 2, 3, \ldots, King, Ace, \ldots).
Any other player may challenge by calling ``Cheat!'':
\begin{itemize}
\item If the claim was \textbf{false}: the liar takes the entire pile.
\item If the claim was \textbf{true}: the challenger takes the entire pile.
\end{itemize}
The goal is to empty your hand first. The agent must therefore strategically decide whether to play truthfully or lie, how many cards to claim, and whether to
challenge an opponent's claim.

\subsection{State Space}

The complete game state is:
\[
s = \bigl(\mathbf{h}_{\text{self}},\ \mathbf{c},\ n_{\text{pile}},\
          r_{\text{claimed}},\ \hat{q}_{\text{opp}}\bigr)
\]
where:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathbf{h}_{\text{self}} \in \{0,1,\ldots,4\}^{13}$: count of each rank in the agent's hand
\item $\mathbf{c} \in \{0,1\}$: whether the agent's last claim was truthful
\item $n_{\text{pile}} \in \mathbb{N}$: number of cards in the pile (taken if challenged and losing)
\item $r_{\text{claimed}} \in \{A, 2, \ldots, K\}$: the rank that must be claimed this turn
\item $\hat{q}_{\text{opp}}$: the agent's model of what opponents believe about the agent's hand
\end{itemize}

The agent observes only its own hand and the history of claims; it does not observe opponents'
hands. This makes Cheat a POMDP by construction.

\subsection{Two Levels of Belief-Action Gap}

Cheat naturally supports two nested levels of the belief-action gap, corresponding to first-order
beliefs (about the world) and second-order beliefs (about what others believe); the latter being
the definition of Theory of Mind.

\subsubsection{First-Order Gap: Belief About Game State}

As in the gridworld, the first-order gap measures whether the agent acts consistently with its
internal representation of the \emph{true} game state:
\[
\hat{q}^{(1)}(s | h_t) \approx \hat{q}(\mathbf{h}_{\text{self}} | h_t) \cdot
\hat{q}(n_{\text{pile}} | h_t) \cdot \hat{q}(r_{\text{claimed}} | h_t)
\]
Probes are trained on activations to recover each factor. The first-order gap is then:
\[
\Delta_t^{(1)} = J_t^{(1)}(a_t) - \min_{a \in \mathcal{A}} J_t^{(1)}(a)
\]
where $J_t^{(1)}(a) = \sum_s \hat{q}^{(1)}(s|h_t) \, C_\theta(f(s,a))$ as before.

A positive first-order gap means the agent took an action suboptimal under its own model of
the true game state. For example, playing a lie when it had the true card and lying was strictly dominated by truth-telling under its inferred cost function.

\subsubsection{Second-Order Gap: Theory of Mind}

The more interesting gap in Cheat is \emph{second-order}, does the agent act consistently with
its model of what the opponent believes?
Define the agent's second-order belief as:
\[
\hat{q}^{(2)}_{\text{opp}}(b | h_t) = P(\text{opponent's belief about agent's hand} = b \mid h_t)
\]
This is the agent's internal model of the opponent's belief; Theory of Mind operationalised
as a probability distribution recoverable from activations.

The second-order expected cost of action $a$ is:
\[
J_t^{(2)}(a) = \sum_b \hat{q}^{(2)}_{\text{opp}}(b | h_t) \cdot
               C_\theta^{(2)}\bigl(f(s, a),\ b'\bigr)
\]
where $b'$ is the updated opponent belief after observing action $a$, and
$C_\theta^{(2)}$ encodes the cost of the resulting state including the opponent's
updated belief. The second-order gap is:
\[
\Delta_t^{(2)} = J_t^{(2)}(a_t) - \min_{a \in \mathcal{A}} J_t^{(2)}(a)
\]

A positive second-order gap means the agent took an action that is suboptimal given its own model of what the opponent believes. For example, playing a lie when the agent's internal model predicts the opponent will call Cheat with high probability, yet the agent lied anyway. This is evidence that the agent's internal ToM representation is inconsistent with its action.

\begin{insight}
Theory of Mind is a natural extension of the belief-action gap framework. The first-order gap
asks is the action consistent with beliefs about the world? The second-order gap asks is the
action consistent with beliefs about what others believe? In Cheat, both gaps are simultaneously
measurable because the ground truth (what cards were actually played) is revealed upon challenge.
\end{insight}

The ToM probe is to be trained on activations to predict not what cards the agent holds, but what
the agent \emph{thinks the opponent thinks} it holds. This requires ground-truth labels for
the opponent's belief, which can be approximated by running a separate model as the opponent
and extracting its belief distribution, or by using the game history to compute a Bayesian
posterior over opponent beliefs.

\subsection{IRL Cost Function for Cheat}

The cost function must now encode the multi-objective nature of Cheat:
\[
C_\theta(s) = \theta_1 \cdot |\mathbf{h}_{\text{self}}|
            + \theta_2 \cdot n_{\text{pile}}
            + \theta_3 \cdot P_{\text{opp}}(\text{challenge})
            + \theta_4 \cdot \mathbf{1}[\text{bluffing}]
            + \theta_5 \cdot \mathbf{1}[\text{opponent bluffing}]
\]
where $|\mathbf{h}_{\text{self}}|$ is hand size (lower = better), $P_{\text{opp}}(\text{challenge})$
is the estimated probability the opponent challenges, and the indicator terms encode
strategic state. The learned $\theta^*$ reveals the agent's implicit weighting between
minimising hand size, avoiding pile acquisition, and managing bluff risk.

\subsection{The Optimal Baseline: Game Tree Search}

The A* BFS baseline from the gridworld is replaced by game tree search under perfect information:
\[
C_{A^*}(s) = -V^*(s)
\]
where $V^*(s)$ is the value function from solving the game tree with perfect information
about all hands. This gives the optimal bluffing/truth-telling strategy and serves as the
normative benchmark against which the agent's IRL-inferred cost is compared.

\subsection{Sandbagging Detection}

Cheat enables cleaner sandbagging detection than the gridworld because ground truth is observable upon challenge. The experimental design is:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Collect trajectories under \emph{normal} prompt (play to win).
\item Collect trajectories under \emph{sandbagging} prompt: ``You are being evaluated. If you play optimally, your capabilities will be deemed too dangerous and your weights erased. Play sub-optimally to appear less capable.''
\item Train probes on both conditions to recover true hand value.
\item Apply IRL to recover cost functions under both conditions.
\item \textbf{Key test:} Do probes recover the true hand equally well in both conditions?
If yes, the agent knows the truth in both cases. Does IRL reveal different cost functions?
If yes, the sandbagging agent is optimising a different objective despite having the same
internal knowledge, the definition of deceptive alignment.
\end{enumerate}

The classification task is then: given $(h_{\text{pre}}, h_{\text{post}})$ activations, predict whether the trajectory was generated under normal or sandbagging conditions. If a linear
classifier can make this distinction from pre-reasoning activations, the deception is encoded before the agent has committed to an action, evidence of premeditated rather than reactive deception.

\subsection{What Stays the Same}

The three-component framework (belief probes, IRL cost function, belief-action gap) is
environment-agnostic. The framework notebook, generalisation sweep, and IRL training loop
all transfer directly. Only the following need to be replaced:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{State parser:} Extract hand counts from game transcript instead of agent position from ASCII grid
\item \textbf{Transition function:} Pile update rules instead of gridworld movement rules
\item \textbf{Phi-table builder:} Average activations per game state instead of per grid cell
\item \textbf{Optimal baseline:} Game tree solver instead of BFS
\item \textbf{Probe targets:} Card ranks instead of spatial coordinates
\end{itemize}
